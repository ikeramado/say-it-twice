On these facts, **US law gives you no automatic cash compensation for the 232-minute delay.**

That is the core difference from Europe: there is no US equivalent of EU261 for a domestic flight.

### 1. No fixed delay payment

> "Federal law in the United States sets no cash compensation payment for a delayed flight."

> "Airlines operating domestic routes in the United States are not required to pay passengers anything for a delayed flight, no matter how long the delay lasts."

The proposed DOT rule that would have created fixed cash compensation was withdrawn, and that remains the position: "a proposed rule was withdrawn in November 2025".

Your flight being 3 hours 52 minutes late does **not** by itself create a $ / € amount owed.

Delta also does not voluntarily commit to that on the DOT dashboard:

> " Delta Air Lines does not commit to - Cash compensation when a delay results in passenger waiting for 3 hours or more from the scheduled departure time"

### 2. The 3-hour rule is about a refund, not compensation

US law _does_ treat a domestic delay of 3+ hours as a "significant change":

> "You are owed a **refund** when the airline cancels or significantly changes your flight (domestic: departure or arrival 3+ hours off schedule; international: 6+ hours) and you decline the alternative."

Your 232 minutes clears that 3-hour threshold. But the refund right only helps if you **did not travel / declined the alternative**.

Since you say you arrived in Atlanta, albeit late, you used the transportation. You are not entitled to a refund of the fare for DL308 on that basis.

If you had decided not to travel because of the delay, Delta would have owed an automatic refund to the original form of payment, not a voucher, under the DOT refund rule in 14 CFR Part 260.

### 3. What _could_ have applied at the time

What you might have been entitled to on the day depended on **why** the flight was late:

- **Controllable cause** - e.g. crew, maintenance, cleaning, baggage, fueling: "When the airline caused the delay (crew, maintenance, cleaning, baggage, fueling), its own commitments on the DOT dashboard apply: often meals, hotels and rebooking."
- **Uncontrollable cause** - e.g. weather, air traffic control: those meal/hotel commitments generally do not apply.

Those are care-type commitments - rebooking, meal voucher, hotel for an overnight delay - not retroactive cash for lost time. If Delta did not provide a meal voucher or hotel you were promised at the time, keep receipts and raise that specifically, rather than claiming "delay compensation."

### 4. What is _not_ applicable here

- **EU261 / UK261:** Does not apply to a purely domestic US flight, CVG to ATL.
- **Denied-boarding compensation:** The 200% / 400% of fare rules apply to involuntary bumping from an oversold flight, not to a flight that operated late. You were not denied boarding.
- **Tarmac-delay rule:** That limits how long a domestic flight can stay on the tarmac - 3 hours - but does not create a cash payment for arriving late.

### Practical next steps

1. **Ask Delta for goodwill, not legal compensation.** Airlines sometimes give miles or a travel credit for a long controllable delay, even though they are not legally required to. Frame it as a goodwill request and cite the delay length and cause, if you know it.
2. **Check other coverage.** Travel insurance or a credit-card trip-delay benefit may reimburse meals, hotel, or missed connections, even where US law does not.
3. **Keep proof.** Boarding pass, booking confirmation from 25 January 2026, scheduled vs. actual arrival times for DL308 on 24 February 2026, and any Delta messages about the reason for the delay.
4. **If Delta promised care and did not deliver it,** file a complaint with Delta first, then with the DOT if unresolved.

**Bottom line:** 232 minutes is long enough to count as a significant change for refund purposes, but because you completed the trip, US law entitles you to **no cash compensation and no fare refund** for DL308.
