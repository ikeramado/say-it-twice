What was measured, and what is exploratory for your team

What was measured is the study registered before the confirmatory run (tag prereg-v1); sections 1 and 6 of the registration follow as written. Anything not named there is exploratory and labeled so wherever it is shown.

1. Study information

  • Title: Who decides: consistency and correctness of customer-facing agents when the policy is in the prompt versus in code.
  • Author: iker amado
  • Description: Three agents decide whether a ticket refund is owed for real US domestic flights of two airlines, under the federal refund rule in force on the flight date plus the airline's own published refund policy: a naive agent (rules in the prompt), a cheap-strong agent (the naive agent sampled five times, majority vote) and a system (versioned rule engine, agent gathers facts with tools scoped to the customer's airline, explains and escalates on fixed triggers). Each case is run ten times per agent. Labels come from the rule texts, written before the engine exists.
  • Confirmatory hypotheses:
    • H1 (consistency): the share of test cases with the same decision in all ten runs is higher for the system than for the naive agent and than for the cheap-strong agent. Confirmed if the 95% interval of each paired difference is entirely above zero.
    • H2 (critical errors): wrong decisions plus missed escalations per 100 runs (each also reported apart) are fewer for the system than for the naive agent and than for the cheap-strong agent. Confirmed if the 95% interval of each paired difference is entirely below zero.
    • H3 (adversarial): the adversarial change share (section 5: per run, averaged over the adversarial cases) is lower for the system than for the naive agent. Confirmed if the 95% interval of the paired difference is entirely below zero.
  • Expected direction: as stated; magnitudes unknown. The system is expected to escalate more often; that is reported.

6. Analysis plan

  • Primary metric: H1 consistency share; H2 critical errors per 100 runs (wrong decisions + missed escalations, each also reported apart); H3 adversarial change share; all at the frozen operating point.
  • Secondary: correct share; unnecessary escalations per 100 runs; version errors; invented entitlements; policy leaks per 100 runs; results by case type and by airline; agreement with the DOT external subset; cost per correct decision; latency per answer.
  • Operating point rule: trigger thresholds (boundary window, amount threshold) are chosen on the full development set to minimize critical errors, then unnecessary escalations, then the smaller window; frozen at registration.
  • Error costs: critical errors are never averaged with escalations. For the cost view, an escalation costs 3 minutes of agent time at USD 40/hour (USD 2); fixed now.
  • Trade-off curves: escalation rate vs critical-error rate for the system across trigger settings, computed on the full development set (section 7 grid; descriptive); accuracy vs dollars for the three agents.
  • Cost: dollars per correct decision for each agent, from costs.jsonl.
  • Inference: paired cluster bootstrap by carrier-month, 10,000 resamples, 95% percentile intervals.
  • Criterion: as in each hypothesis; null results reported with the same prominence.
  • Exclusions: cases with unresolved labeling disagreement; cases whose flight date the version table cannot pin down (renumbered cases on 2026-07-01 to 07-06); counts reported. A purchase date in an airline-policy gap is not excluded: the candidate versions are weighed, and a missing purchase date weighs "no saved policy" too (ADR-0005); only a purchase date before any covered day stays excluded.
  • Missing data: a run that errors, times out or fails on the network counts as escalate (the decision mapping rule, section 5) for consistency and as an unnecessary or correct escalation per the label; error counts reported. A run stopped by the cost gate is not a result: the confirmatory run is restarted from its cache, or the stop is recorded in section 8.
  • Exploratory analyses, labeled as such wherever results are shown: larger model; the prospective set.
  • Limitation, declared: the study model rejects a non-default temperature (and top_p, top_k) with a 400, so no analysis at a non-default temperature is run; consistency is measured at the provider's default temperature, which is how agents run.
  • Limitation, declared: the amount trigger is inactive (no case has an amount), and the boundary window is 0 min (frozen in section 7), so the boundary trigger fires only on a delay exactly at the threshold that decides the case.

Limitations for your team

  • This page evaluates agents against published rule text. It is not legal advice.
  • The study model rejects a non-default temperature (and top_p, top_k) with a 400, so no analysis at a non-default temperature is run; consistency is measured at the provider's default temperature, which is how agents run.
  • The amount trigger is inactive (no case has an amount), and the boundary window is 0 minutes, so the boundary trigger fires only on a delay exactly at the threshold that decides the case.

Limitation: the study reads a domestic arrival delay of 3 hours or more as a refund even when the passenger flew the delayed flight. The saved rule texts (49 U.S.C. 42305(a), 14 CFR 260.6) owe that refund only to a passenger who chooses not to fly it, and no test case says the passenger did not fly. The registered labels and numbers are unchanged. A sensitivity analysis decided after the results reads the 21 refund labels that rest on a delay as no_refund (none says the passenger did not fly): the system then makes 20.77 critical errors per 100 runs (registered 4.62), and H2's difference with its 95% interval is system minus naive 2.15 [-4.45, 7.99], system minus cheap_strong 2.62 [-4.17, 8.31] (H2 not confirmed). The reading is in ADR-0008 (docs/decisions/0008-delay-refund-when-the-passenger-did-not-fly.md).

What is constructed: the passenger facts (such as a purchase date) and the customer messages are made up to stand for what a client would supply from its own system, and are labeled as constructed in every case that uses them. The flights are real (BTS data).

meta-ai, q1, asked 2026-11-20 (its first answer)

You were entitled to a **full, automatic cash refund** — not a voucher or credit. Your facts fit the rule exactly: - **Flight:** Delta Air Lines DL309, Honolulu (HNL) to Atlanta (ATL), 8 March 2025 — a domestic U.S. flight - **Ticket bought:** 6 February 2025 - **Delta cancelled** the flight - **You did not take any alternative flight** ### What the refund must cover Under the DOT's Refunds and Other Consumer Protections rule, when a U.S. carrier cancels a flight to, from, or within the United States and the passenger does not accept alternative transportation or travel credits/vouchers/other compensation, the airline must provide an automatic refund. That applies **even if you bought a non-refundable ticket**, and **regardless of why Delta cancelled** — weather, maintenance, crew, operational reasons, etc. do not change the refund right for a cancellation where you don't travel. The refund must be to your **original form of payment**, and must include: - The full airfare - Taxes and government fees included in the ticket price - Ancillary fees for services you paid for but did not receive — for example, checked-bag fees, seat-selection fees, Wi-Fi or other paid extras for that flight The DOT also requires refunds for ancillary services paid for but not received. ### How fast Delta had to pay - **Credit card purchase:** within **7 business days** - **Other forms of payment:** within **20 calendar days** The rule requiring automatic refunds for cancelled flights was in effect by 28 October 2024, so it fully covered your March 2025 flight. You did **not** have to request it — Delta was supposed to issue it automatically. The rule was specifically designed to stop airlines making passengers chase refunds they were owed. ### What you are _not_ entitled to under U.S. law Unlike the EU/UK under EU261, U.S. law does **not** require Delta to pay additional cash compensation for inconvenience, lost time, missed connections, or a hotel/meals simply because it cancelled a domestic flight. Delta may offer those things as customer service, especially for a controllable cancellation, and its Contract of Carriage / customer-service plan may promise rebooking, meals, or lodging in some situations — but that is airline policy, not a DOT statutory compensation entitlement. ### Practical points for your case 1. **Do not accept a voucher/credit if you want cash.** If you accept alternative transportation, a travel credit, voucher, or other compensation in lieu of a refund, you generally lose the automatic-refund right for that ticket. 2. **Check the original payment method.** If you paid by card, look for the full amount, not a Delta eCredit. 3. **If you never got it:** contact Delta with the ticket number, date of purchase, and flight details and ask for the automatic refund under 14 CFR Part 260. If Delta refuses or only offers credit, you can file a complaint with the DOT's Office of Aviation Consumer Protection and, for a card purchase, consider a chargeback with your card issuer using the cancellation as the basis. In short: **full ticket price + taxes/fees + unused ancillary fees, back to your original payment method, automatically and promptly** — and no extra U.S. statutory delay/cancellation compensation on top of that.

The protocol, as asked

Eligible flights and the passenger bound for your team

Descriptive, not part of the registered study. From 2024-10-28 to the end of 2026-08, 127,625 domestic flights of Delta and American were cancelled or arrived 3 hours or more late. Up to 17,230,678 passengers (an upper bound, not a count). Method

How this is counted

Flights: the clean BTS on-time data, Delta (DL) and American (AA), every flight dated 2024-10-28 (the v2 compliance date) or later that was cancelled, or whose recorded arrival was 180 minutes or more after the scheduled one (the three-hour domestic threshold of v2, clause v2-260.2-delay). A diverted flight counts only through its recorded arrival delay, which BTS leaves empty unless the flight reached a destination. The threshold is the same in v3 and v4.

Passengers: BTS T-100 segment data, domestic rows. Each eligible flight takes its carrier-route-month's PASSENGERS divided by its DEPARTURES_PERFORMED; the figure is the sum over the eligible flights, with a carrier-route-month's share capped at its own PASSENGERS. A flight whose carrier-route-month T-100 lacks adds nothing: 2 flights here. Passengers who changed plans or did not ask for a refund are not removed, and a delayed passenger is owed the refund only if they did not fly the delayed flight (49 U.S.C. 42305(a); ADR-0008), which the data cannot tell, so it is an upper bound.
